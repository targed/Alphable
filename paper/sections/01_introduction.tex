\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Imperfect-information games have long served as primary testing grounds for artificial intelligence. While complete-information games such as Chess, Go, and Shogi have yielded to deep reinforcement learning and Monte Carlo Tree Search \citep{silver2016mastering, silver2017mastering}, environments characterized by hidden private states demand fundamentally different algorithmic formulations \citep{moravcik2017deepstack, brown2019superhuman, sokota2026scalable}. In imperfect-information settings, an agent must act without direct observation of opponent resources, evaluating prospective choices over dynamic probability distributions of hidden states while balancing short-term scoring, long-term defensive equity, and information disclosure.

The game of Scrabble provides an exceptionally rich imperfect-information benchmark. Unlike Poker, which pairs private cards with relatively small discrete betting choices (fold, call, raise), Scrabble combines private information sets with massive combinatorial action branching. At any given turn, an agent selects from hundreds to tens of thousands of valid legal placements across a $15 \times 15$ grid governed by strict lexical constraints, such as the Collins Scrabble Words (CSW24) or North American School Words (NWL23) lexica. Each player holds a private rack of up to seven tiles drawn without replacement from a finite bag of 100 tiles, while unplayed letters remain hidden until committed to the board. Replenishment draws introduce continuous environmental stochasticity governed by hypergeometric distributions, and every placed word permanently transforms board topology by opening or closing premium multiplier squares, hook letters, and rack-dumping lanes for the opponent.

\subsection{Existing Paradigms and the Simulation Dilemma}

Competitive Scrabble engines have historically relied on two dominant computational paradigms. The first paradigm, pioneered by systems such as Maven \citep{sheppard2002world} and modern high-speed C engines such as default \magpie, evaluates candidate plays using a 1-ply static equity heuristic:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:static_equity}
    \equity(a) = \score(a) + \leaveval(\rack \setminus a)
\end{equation}
Here, $\score(a)$ denotes the immediate placement score and $\leaveval$ is precomputed through offline Monte Carlo simulations of prospective tile draws, known as Komodo Leave Values (KLV or KLV2). Although this formulation is exceptionally fast, generating candidates in less than a millisecond, it operates myopically: it possesses no tactical lookahead, ignores opponent counterplay threats, and evaluates rack leaves independently of the board configuration.

The second paradigm relies on Perfect Information Monte Carlo (PIMC) simulation, popularized by engines such as \quackle\ \citep{olaughlin2006quackle} and \macondo\ \citep{del2022macondo}. PIMC samples determinized opponent racks and simulates deep rollouts to estimate win probabilities. However, PIMC suffers from the well-documented strategy fusion flaw \citep{sokota2026scalable}: determinizing private states during search implicitly assumes that the agent will possess clairvoyant knowledge of opponent tiles on future plies. Consequently, PIMC engines systematically overvalue speculative defensive maneuvers that attempt to block specific opponent bingos, while underestimating robust offensive plays that succeed across the true unobserved distribution.

This tension exposes a fundamental question in imperfect-information game AI: Can learned neural valuation and belief-consistent tracking provide an authentic strategic advantage over the raw tactical lookahead of compiled Monte Carlo simulation engines that generate nearly $100,000$ rollouts per second?

\subsection{Research Evolution and Empirical Findings}

Our investigation proceeded through four iterative phases. In the initial phase, we developed a 28-channel spatial board tensor coupled with a ResNet-18 convolutional backbone and cross-attention rack query embeddings, integrated into an asymmetric 2-ply search algorithm termed Belief-Augmented Monte Carlo Search (\bams). Evaluated across 10,000 paired duplicate tournament games (5,000 duplicate pairs) against 1-ply static heuristic \magpie, 2-ply \bams\ demonstrated a decisive $+7.06 \pm 0.81$ net point advantage per game ($p = 2.90 \times 10^{-18}$, $t = 8.75$).

In the second phase, we conducted an adversarial parameter audit of native Base \magpie. We discovered that default benchmark modes disable the engine's most powerful native capabilities: compiled PIMC simulation rollouts operating at $\sim 98,134$ rollouts per second, Positional Adjustment Terms (\texttt{-pat CSW24}), and an exact minimax endgame solver. In exploratory 40-game preliminary probes against this Maximally Tuned configuration, standalone 2-ply \bams\ fell behind, revealing that shallow 2-ply neural lookahead cannot match deep simulation rollouts in complex midgame positions.

In the third phase, we analyzed the statistical mechanics of Scrabble evaluation. In competitive duplicate play, single-game standard deviation is substantial ($\sigma \approx 105.7$ points per pair). In a 20-pair (40-game) tournament, the standard error of the mean is $\text{SEM} \approx 23.6$ points per pair, producing $95\%$ confidence intervals that span $\pm 46.3$ points. We proved that small-scale matches yield noise-dominated swings that mislead algorithmic conclusions, necessitating multi-thousand-game tournaments to verify sub-point effect sizes.

In the fourth phase, we developed \algo\ Hybrid PIMC, fusing native C simulation rollouts with deep cross-attention candidate re-ranking, Bayesian negative-evidence belief tracking, and exact late-game solvers. By resolving cluster multiprocessing memory limits and compiler compatibility challenges, we executed a confirmatory 10,000-game (5,000 duplicate pairs) tournament on the Missouri S\&T High-Performance Computing Cluster across 24 AMD EPYC processor cores. Under this rigorous evaluation, \algo\ Hybrid PIMC defeated Maximally Tuned \magpie\ with a win rate of $53.30\%$ and an average net spread of $+4.15$ points per game ($95\%$ CI: $[+2.68, +5.61]$ points, total net $+41,474$ points, paired $t = 5.5517$, $p = 2.83 \times 10^{-8}$), surpassing the 5.5-sigma discovery threshold.

\subsection{Summary of Scientific Contributions}

The primary scientific contributions of this work are summarized as follows. First, we design a deep cross-attention value network that dynamically models the interaction between spatial board configurations and unplayed rack letters, trained via Huber loss on 500,000 positions. Second, we introduce Bayesian negative-evidence opponent belief tracking, which updates hidden rack probabilities when an opponent bypasses high-scoring scoring lanes, eliminating ghost-threat paralysis. Third, we establish the Hybrid PIMC architecture, which unifies high-throughput compiled simulation rollouts with learned neural candidate re-ranking. Fourth, we provide extensive empirical validation across 10,000 duplicate tournament games on supercomputing infrastructure, decomposing opening initiative from second-mover defense and demonstrating that neural evaluation provides strategic value that raw simulation throughput alone cannot achieve. Finally, we make the complete \algo\ codebase, neural weights, and tournament logs publicly available under the GPLv3 license to establish a reproducible benchmark for imperfect-information game AI.
