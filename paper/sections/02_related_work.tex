\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

\subsection{Lexical Generation and Word Graph Automata}

Modern Scrabble search depends on fast move generation over directed acyclic word graphs. \citet{appel1988world} introduced the Directed Acyclic Word Graph (DAWG), converting lexical prefix lookups across the board into linear graph traversals. \citet{gordon1994faster} introduced the GADDAG data structure, which traverses valid words bidirectionally outward from existing anchor squares. Competitive engines, including \magpie\ and \macondo\ \citep{del2022macondo}, use Kurn Word Graphs (KWG) and compacted GADDAGs capable of generating millions of valid placements per second in compiled code. \algo\ uses \magpie's C GADDAG generator as its underlying move generation library.

\subsection{Classical Scrabble AI and Static Leave Values}

\citet{sheppard2002world} established the architecture of competitive computer Scrabble in Maven. Maven combined 1-ply candidate generation with tabular rack leave values (the Komodo Leave Values, KLV), computed through offline Monte Carlo simulation of millions of prospective tile draws. Maven also introduced 1-ply opponent lookahead (termed B-1 search) to anticipate immediate opponent replies.

\citet{olaughlin2006quackle} formalized \quackle, an open-source C++ engine that became the standard research platform in Scrabble AI. \quackle\ introduced Perfect Information Monte Carlo (PIMC) simulation to estimate win percentages rather than point margins alone. \citet{del2022macondo} developed \macondo\ in Go, incorporating superleaf leave tables (KLV2), B* search heuristic pruning, and multi-core candidate simulations.

Contemporary high-throughput engines such as \magpie\ implement KLV2 leave valuation alongside optional Positional Adjustment Terms (PAT). In default benchmark configurations, however, \magpie\ functions as a purely static 1-ply evaluator, choosing moves that maximize immediate score plus static leave value without dynamic multi-ply lookahead or opponent modeling.

\subsection{Imperfect-Information Game AI and Search}

Imperfect-information games have seen substantial progress in recent years. In Heads-Up and Multiplayer No-Limit Texas Hold'em, DeepStack \citep{moravcik2017deepstack} and Pluribus \citep{brown2019superhuman} achieved expert-level performance using Counterfactual Regret Minimization (CFR) and continual resolving of public subgames. However, Poker features small discrete action sets at each decision point (such as fold, call, or raise), in contrast to Scrabble's combinatorial action branching of $10^3$ to $10^4$ valid word placements.

In large-scale board games with hidden information, \citet{sokota2026scalable} introduced Ataraxos for Stratego, a game characterized by hidden piece ranks and long game horizons. \citet{sokota2026scalable} analyzed the pathology of the strategy fusion flaw in PIMC search: determinized rollouts assume that an agent will know hidden opponent states on future turns, leading to systematic defensive errors. They demonstrated that combining neural policy and value networks with belief-consistent search provides a scalable design pattern across imperfect-information games. In structuring our experimental procedures, verification gates, and reporting standards, we adhere to the reproducible research methodology formulated by \citet{kassis2026scientific}.

\algo\ builds upon these game-theoretic insights. We combine high-speed native C simulation rollouts with deep cross-attention positional valuation and Bayesian belief tracking, addressing the strategy fusion flaw while maintaining the throughput required for tournament time controls.
